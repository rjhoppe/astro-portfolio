### Intro
One of the biggest surprises I encountered when starting CPAP therapy was how my treatment impacted my dental health. Here are some things to keep an eye when beginning your CPAP journey so you don't damage your beautiful pearly whites.

### What You Might Experience
CPAP therapy is a shock to your body, especially when starting out. After potentially years of coping with a lack of oxygen during sleep, your body is now having a continuous stream of air, blasted down its airways at various degrees of pressure all night long. This is an aggressive (but effective!) way of treating sleep apnea. And as such your body may display signs that it's not full onboard with this whole therapy thing.

#### Teeth Grinding
One of the first things I noticed when starting CPAP therapy is that I started to REALLY grind my teeth. I believe I had a tendancy to do it occassionally prior to starting CPAP therapy, but it was never something I needed formally treated by my dentist. This grinding was so intense that I was grinding through the retainers I wear at night. 

From my understanding, wearing a chinstrap (like I do) can exacerbate this. It really depends on how tight you adjust your chinstrap and how the chinstrap orients your jaw when it is engaged. I use the Resmed Premium Chin Strap and really have to make it pretty snug to prevent myself from mouthbreathing, which I believe contributed to my teeth grinding.

#### Dry Mouth
Something else you may start to notice is regularly waking up with "cottonmouth" AKA "dry mouth." This can introduce a whole host of dental issues if left untreated. Issues such as: tooth sensitivity, plaque build up, tooth erosion, and cavities. In severe cases it can also contribute to tooth decay and gum disease.

### What You Should Consider Doing...
1. Tell your dentist that you have started CPAP therapy

Your dentist might not know to look for signs of CPAP related damage to your teeth if he doesn't know you have started therapy! Signs of damage associated with CPAP therapy could be assumed to be from normal wear and tear unless you give the dentist reason to suspect otherwise. It's also better to catch these issues sooner rather than later to avoid irreversible damage.

2. Look into getting a nightguard
If you are having trouble getting your teeth grinding under control, it might time for defensive action. Ask your dentist about a nightguard (more formally known as an "occlusal guard").

I eventually needed to get a nightguard from my dentist to treat this issue. It ran me about $800, which I had to pay out of pocket because this type of applicance falls into a weird insurance gray area. Medical insurnace considers this to be an issue that should be treated by dental insurance and dental insurance considers it to be something that should be treated by medical insurance. YMMV - but from my research at the time, I found that this device is very difficult to get covered by either form of insurance. You can opt to go for an OTC night guard option, but my research led me to the conclusion that many of those options can cause just as many long term dental problems as they fix. You can really mess your bite up with those or flat out fail to properly addressing grinding altogether. You will have to do your own due diligence and make the best decision for yourself given your circumstances and resources.

Despite being expensive, the nightguard has worked really well at preventing damage from teeth grinding, while also offering me peace of mind while I fall sleep. I found that worrying about damaging my teeth had become a significant stressor that was impacting my ability to relax enough to fall asleep. For me, I would say it was worth it because it was one less thing to worry about as I was trying to adapt to therapy.

3. Address the recurring "cotton mouth" (dry mouth) phenomenon if you notice it becoming a trend.

Dry mouth is typically caused by mouthbreathing. There's no silver bullet to fixing mouthbreathing, but here are some of the most popular strategies:

* Full-face mask
* Humidifier
* Chinstrap
* Mouth tape
* Side sleeping

If you are a mouthbreather (or at least breathe through your mouth for substantial portions of the nigth), the lowest hanging fruit is to switch to a full-face mask and use a humidifier if you are not already doing so. I resisted the full-face mask for far too long due to it's perception of not being comfortable. But do you know what is much more uncomfortable? Having ineffective therapy and not being able to sleep well! Seriously, switching to a full-face mask profoundly improved my therapy because I was a mouthbreather.
